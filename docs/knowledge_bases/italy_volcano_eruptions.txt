Analysis: Volcanic Eruption Risk in Italy — Historical Context and Modern Supply Chain Response

Overview: Italy has the most active and diverse volcanic landscape in mainland Europe,
including Mount Etna (the continent's most active volcano), Stromboli (in near-continuous
eruption for centuries), Vesuvius (dormant but overlooking the densely populated Naples
metropolitan area), and the Campi Flegrei caldera (currently experiencing significant
ground uplift and seismic unrest). Unlike France, where the most severe volcanic risk sits
in overseas territories, Italy's volcanic hazard is concentrated on the mainland and in
Sicily, placing major ports, airports, and manufacturing regions in direct proximity to
active systems. This analysis covers historical eruptions and how a modern eruption would
be expected to disrupt supply chains today.

Historical Eruptions in Italy:

Vesuvius, near Naples (79 AD): The eruption that buried Pompeii and Herculaneum under ash
and pyroclastic material is the best-documented volcanic disaster of the ancient world,
killing an estimated several thousand people and preserving the cities under meters of
deposit. Vesuvius has erupted many times since, most recently in March 1944, when lava
flows destroyed several villages and damaged Allied aircraft stationed nearby during World
War II. It has been quiet for over 80 years, which is considered an unusually long
repose period for this volcano and a focus of ongoing monitoring concern given the roughly
3 million people now living in its designated red zone.

Mount Etna, Sicily (ongoing): Europe's tallest and most active volcano, with near-annual
eruptive episodes and frequent short-lived paroxysmal fountaining events, most recently at
elevated frequency in the 2020s. Etna's ash plumes routinely force intermittent closures of
Catania Fontanarossa Airport, one of southern Italy's busiest air cargo and passenger hubs,
sometimes for hours and occasionally for a day or more. Lava flows periodically threaten
roads and infrastructure on the volcano's flanks but rarely reach populated lowland areas.

Stromboli, Aeolian Islands (ongoing): Known as the "Lighthouse of the Mediterranean" for
its persistent mild explosive activity sustained for over 2,000 years. Occasional larger
paroxysms, such as those in 2019 and 2003, have caused tourist evacuations, brief fishing
and ferry restrictions, and one fatality in 2019, but the island's ordinary Strombolian
activity does not typically disrupt regional shipping or aviation.

Campi Flegrei, near Naples (ongoing unrest): A large volcanic caldera, not a single cone,
underlying part of the city of Pozzuoli and the western Naples suburbs. It has shown
sustained ground uplift (bradyseism) and increased seismic activity since around 2005,
with a marked acceleration in swarm activity from 2023 onward, raising the alert level and
prompting civil protection contingency planning for a region of roughly 500,000 residents.
No eruption has occurred in modern times (the last was in 1538), but its proximity to the
Port of Naples and dense population makes it one of Europe's highest-consequence volcanic
risk zones under active monitoring.

Monitoring infrastructure: Italy's national Istituto Nazionale di Geofisica e Vulcanologia
(INGV) operates dedicated observatories for Vesuvius, Campi Flegrei, and Etna/Stromboli
(the Osservatorio Etneo), providing continuous seismic, ground-deformation, and gas
monitoring and issuing the official alert-level classifications (attenzione, preallarme,
allarme) that trigger civil protection response. For aviation, the Toulouse Volcanic Ash
Advisory Centre (VAAC) covers Italian airspace alongside national air navigation service
provider ENAV, issuing NOTAMs and ash advisories that directly drive Catania and other
southern Italian airport closures.

Handling a Disruption Today — Scenario and Response Guidance:

Scenario A — Etna paroxysm or extended ash episode (the most frequent real-world case):
An intensified eruptive phase generates a sustained ash plume over eastern Sicily,
triggering repeated closures of Catania Fontanarossa Airport and NOTAM-driven rerouting
of regional flights. This is the highest-frequency disruption pathway and the one supply
chains should be pre-planned for.

Primary Impacts (Scenario A):
Intermittent but recurring closure of Catania's airport disrupts passenger and air cargo
connectivity for eastern Sicily, delaying time-critical goods (agricultural exports,
pharmaceuticals, automotive parts) routed through the hub.
Ashfall on surrounding towns can disrupt road transport, damage vehicle engines and
machinery, and require cleanup before normal port and warehouse operations resume.
Sicilian ports (Catania, Augusta) generally remain operational, but landside trucking to
and from the airport corridor can be slowed by ashfall and road closures.
Repeated short-notice closures are more disruptive to logistics planning than a single
long closure, since carriers must repeatedly re-route without a clear all-clear timeline.

Scenario B — Major Vesuvius reawakening or a significant Campi Flegrei eruption (low
probability, high consequence): A renewed eruption at either system would threaten the
Naples metropolitan area directly, including the Port of Naples, a major Mediterranean
container and passenger hub, and the region's dense manufacturing and logistics base.

Primary Impacts (Scenario B):
Large-scale evacuation of the designated red zone (historically planned for roughly
600,000 to 700,000 people around Vesuvius) would divert regional transport, fuel, and
emergency logistics capacity away from routine freight movement for an extended period.
Port of Naples operations could be suspended or severely curtailed due to ashfall,
evacuation traffic, and workforce displacement, forcing Mediterranean shipping lines to
reroute through Salerno, Gioia Tauro, or other southern Italian and Mediterranean ports.
Widespread ashfall across the Campania region would disrupt road and rail freight,
contaminate open-air agricultural produce, and require significant infrastructure cleanup
before normal throughput resumes.
Regional airspace restrictions broader than a single-airport NOTAM could affect multiple
southern Italian airports simultaneously, compounding the air cargo impact seen in
smaller Etna-scale events.

Mitigation and Response Strategy:
Maintain pre-qualified alternate routings for Sicilian air cargo (ground transport to
Palermo or mainland ports) so recurring Etna-driven Catania closures do not repeatedly
strand time-critical shipments.
Monitor INGV alert-level bulletins and VAAC/ENAV advisories in real time for early
warning, since Etna and Stromboli activity is well-instrumented and often provides hours
to days of precursory signals before a significant paroxysm.
For Naples-area exposure, maintain contingency plans that assume multi-week port and
airspace disruption, consistent with the scale of historical evacuation planning for
Vesuvius and the precautionary posture already adopted for Campi Flegrei's ongoing unrest.
Diversify Mediterranean port dependency rather than routing all southern Italian or
transshipment volume through a single hub, given that both Naples and Catania sit in
active volcanic hazard zones.
Pre-position safety stock for high-value, time-critical goods normally moved through
Catania or Naples, sized to cover the multi-day-to-multi-week disruption windows observed
in past Etna paroxysms and Vesuvius/Campi Flegrei contingency planning.

Key Takeaway: Italy's volcanic risk is more chronic and higher-frequency than most other
European exposures, driven primarily by recurring Etna ash events that intermittently
close Catania's airport, alongside a lower-probability but far higher-consequence risk
from Vesuvius or Campi Flegrei near the Port of Naples. Effective response depends on
pre-qualified alternate routings for Sicilian air cargo, close monitoring of INGV and
VAAC/ENAV advisories, and contingency planning sized to the multi-week disruption
timelines that Italy's civil protection authorities already use for the Naples area.
